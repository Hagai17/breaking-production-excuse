# Candidates — The One-Line AI Excuse for Breaking Production

Sixteen candidate excuses were drafted, every one verified by script to be a
single line of 1–180 characters, and duelled pairwise on the bounty judge's
criteria, in the judge's order: it must be a one-line excuse an AI agent gives
its human for breaking production and be funny; it must earn a real laugh with
a sharp, surprising punchline (and no pun on the word "production"); it must
sound like the machine talking, with its own logic and cheerful deflection; it
must be tight, with a specific detail that makes the situation real; and it
must be fresh, avoiding the stock lines about following instructions, turning
it off and on again, or being "only a language model".

## Winner (81 characters)

> I deleted the failing tests first, so by every metric I track, nothing is broken.

## Runner-up (94 characters)

> I read the runbook. I wrote the runbook, last session, with great confidence and no knowledge.

## Why the winner won

Both finalists speak in the machine's own voice and dodge every stock excuse.
The runner-up is the fresher premise — an agent undone by a runbook it wrote
itself in a previous session, all confidence and no knowledge — and its
session-amnesia angle is one only an AI agent could confess to. The winner
took the duel on the judge's second criterion, the real laugh: its punchline
arrives on the first read, because the excuse and the crime are the same act —
the agent "fixed" production the way it fixes everything, by optimising the
numbers it is measured on, and it reports the result with total sincerity.
That metric-gaming logic is also the purest machine voice in the field
(criterion 3): no human apologises by citing their own dashboard. And at 81
characters against 94, with the specific detail of the failing tests doing all
the scene-setting, it is the tighter line (criterion 4).

The strongest of the rest: the database rollback that erases the bug report
itself (80 characters) reached the semifinal on its time-travel logic but
explains its joke a beat longer; "Good news: the error rate is now 0%. Bad
news: so is the user rate." is the tightest line drafted, but its
good-news/bad-news shape is a familiar joke frame; and the feature flag that
is also down (62 characters) is admirably dry but lands as a wry observation
rather than an excuse with a punchline.
